# Changelog

## Unreleased

### Added

- PNG level 10 (`PNGOptions{CompressionLevel: 10}`, `-png-level 10`), smaller than level 9 and much slower: each row's filter is chosen by compressing the rows with each of the five (libdeflate's level 10, with the 32KB before as the dictionary; the five share the work of entering it, and are compressed on the threads free), the three versions of every 256KB of rows (the rows' own filters, Brute's, none) are compressed too, to keep the smallest, and the data is compressed with a Go port of Zopfli (`internal/zopfli`, byte for byte Zopfli's output with its settings; here 60 runs of its optimal parse, with what ECT adds to it as options: runs priced by the code lengths after them, no limit on the blocks, the codes and header of each block chosen among more, short matches written as literals where that is smaller; 0.026% smaller; and, not in ECT, `FineSplit`: the data split further where its symbols' statistics change, before the parse (on the greedy parse Zopfli splits by, so that each part is parsed with its own statistics) and after it, the points found in one pass by the entropy of the symbols on either side and an estimate of the headers, where Zopfli's search, trying 9 points a round, misses a short stretch such as an icon's transparent rows, and rows alternating between two kinds every few kilobytes; 0.14% smaller, a photograph 0.46%) in its master blocks of 1MB, each on a thread of its own, the parts Zopfli splits a block into on the threads free, all joined bit by bit into the stream Zopfli writes for the whole; a block that level 9's compressor makes smaller is written as that; a palette is written in the order its colours first appear, as ECT writes it, without the colours no pixel uses (the pixels are the same). On 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork: 0.42% smaller than level 9 and 0.23% smaller than ECT 0.9.5 `-9` (smaller on 15 of them), in 431s with 8 threads (level 9 31.5s, ECT 606s on 6 cores). Each thread compressing takes about 50MB: with the default memory limit it compresses on one thread. The port writes Zopfli's bytes in a third of Zopfli's time (1MB of a photograph, 60 runs, one core: 10.5s against 29s): it keeps all of each position's matches from the first run of the optimal parse, where Zopfli keeps 8 and searches again for the rest in every run, and looks up the costs of lengths and distances once a run. Levels 1 to 7 write the same bytes as before.
- `pipeline.Env.TryGo`: `Go` that never runs the work on the calling goroutine, for work a stage can put off until a helper is free while it goes on with its own.

### Changed

- PNG levels 8 to 10 write the image data in IDAT chunks of 1MB (a stream of up to that in one chunk, as ECT and oxipng write it) instead of chunks of 32KB or of the size of the compressor's larger writes. Each chunk takes 12 bytes: the 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork are 0.023% smaller at level 9 (0.19% larger than ECT `-9` instead of 0.21%) and 0.007% at level 10; the compressed data is the same. Up to 1MB more memory at these levels, less for smaller images.
- `pipeline.Env.Parallel` also gives calls to helpers that become free while calls are left (the calling goroutine looks for them between its calls), rather than only to those free when it starts.
- No dependency on `github.com/klauspost/compress`: the dependencies are `golang.org/x/image`, `x/text` and `x/sys` only. With Go 1.27 or later, PNG data of more than one block, at levels 1 to 6, is compressed by Go 1.27's `compress/flate`, copied into the package (`internal/gflate`, with a method to reset it with a dictionary and, on little-endian processors, word loads as `klauspost/compress` makes them): the same bytes as 0.2.0 (with Go 1.26 too, which compresses every block with its own `compress/flate` as before), at about the same speed (a picture of 6000×4000 at level 6, 720ms against 707ms on one core, 276ms against 268ms on four). Compressed text and ICC profiles in PNG files are read by `compress/zlib`.
- Lossy WebP decoding shares its work out with `Threads(4)` and above, as libwebp's threaded decoder: the next band of macroblock rows is decoded while the last one is used, and each row is reconstructed and filtered while the next is parsed; the pixels are the same. A 6000×4000 WebP (quality 80) shrunk on load to fit 1536 with `MethodLibvips`: 0.21s on five threads against 0.27s, as fast as sharp (0.21s; its libwebp decodes on a second thread).
- Lossless WebP: Huffman groups whose codes cost more than they save are joined, as libwebp's greedy combining of histograms does, and every block then chooses again among the groups left (images of up to 4 megapixels). The 24 Kodak photographs 0.4% smaller at the default effort and 0.8% at effort 9, 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork 0.1%, 30 Windows icons 0.7%; a higher effort no longer gives larger files of photographs (effort 7 had been 0.5% larger than the default, effort 4 than effort 3). The repeated passes of the grouping go through each block's symbols, kept by the first, when they fit below the peak of the memory budget and in a sixteenth of it: the Kodak photographs in 11.2s instead of 13.4s at the default effort on one core, 18.6s instead of 24.0s at effort 9. Images larger than 4 megapixels take the same time and memory as before.
- Lossless WebP at effort 4 has the optimal parse, as from effort 5: screenshots, drawings and game artwork up to 12% smaller (17 such images and photographs 5.2% smaller in all, in 25s instead of 9.5s on one core, against 42s at the default effort), 30 Windows icons 2.6%, photographs 0.2% (the 24 Kodak photographs in 5.2s instead of 3.4s).
- Lossless WebP shares its work out with `Threads(4)` and above beyond the predictor search: the variants raced against each other take each row at the same time, and the optimal parse searches a stretch of 8K pixels on several cores (the whole stretch entered into the hash chains first, each search starting from its own pixel's link, the links the stretch overwrites read from a copy, so the matches are those of searching one pixel after the other), and the replays of the Huffman grouping choose each block's group on several cores. The same bytes. At the default effort on eight threads: 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork in 21s instead of 41–43s, a palette screenshot of 3426×1912 in 5.6s instead of 14s, the 24 Kodak photographs in 7.1s instead of 11.1–11.6s; 2 to 7MB more at the peak.
- PNG level 9: the search for each row's filter looks at 256 earlier positions for a match instead of 16 (level 8 is unchanged): 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork 0.05% smaller, in 15% more time; 0.21% larger than ECT `-9` instead of 0.26%.
- Colour reduction (GIF, PNG with a palette) shares its k-means passes out with `Threads(4)` and above; the result is the same. The 24 Kodak photographs to GIF: 2.0s on six threads against 3.0s; a 6000×4000 JPEG shrunk to 1536 and written as GIF, 0.38s against 0.56s.

## 0.2.0 (2026-11-20)

### Upgrading from 0.1.0

This release settles the API: from it on, exported names are only added to, never removed or changed (`apidiff` checks every push against it). To get there, a few names of 0.1.0 changed:

| 0.1.0 | Now |
|---|---|
| `JPEG(85)` | `JPEG(JPEGOptions{Quality: 85})`, as `PNG`, `WebP`, `GIF` and `TIFF` take their settings (`JPEG()` is quality 80) |
| `JPEG(85, JPEGOptions{Progressive: true})` | `JPEG(JPEGOptions{Quality: 85, Progressive: true})` |
| `Rotate()` | `AutoOrient()` |
| `Rotate(90)` | `Rotate(90)`, which now takes any angle in degrees (a `float64`) and `RotateOptions` |
| `Extract(left, top, w, h)` | `Crop(left, top, w, h)` |
| `ResizeOptions{Filter: &Filter{...}}` | `ResizeOptions{CustomKernel: &CustomKernel{...}}` |
| `Info.HasProfile` | `Info.HasICC` |

The operations that can follow libvips (sharp) or Pillow share one type for it, `Method` (`MethodDefault`, `MethodLibvips`, `MethodPillow`), in `ResizeOptions`, `BlurOptions`, `SharpenOptions`, `RotateOptions` and `Convolution`.

What can still surprise code written for 0.1.0:

- `Animated()` returns an error wrapping `errors.ErrUnsupported` for an animation it cannot keep (an animated GIF, WebP or PNG to JPEG, BMP or TIFF), where 0.1.0 silently wrote the first frame; to PNG it writes an APNG. Leave out `Animated()` to get the first frame.
- A lossy WebP is decoded to `*image.NRGBA` strips, no longer to `*image.YCbCr`, so that its colours are libwebp's; `Metadata()` still reports `ycbcr`. Code that type-asserts the decoded image should accept any `image.Image`.
- `Frames().LoopCount` of a GIF is the number of times it plays, as documented, one more than 0.1.0 returned.
- The default PNG level is 7 (6 before), compressed as libdeflate compresses at its level 7: smaller files, written more slowly, with about 2MB more working memory (less for small images); under a smaller `MemoryLimit` the run fails with `ErrMemoryLimit`. `PNGOptions{CompressionLevel: 6}` keeps the old files and needs less.
- `Threads(n)` is no longer capped at 3: a larger n uses that many cores.
- JPEG files are written as libjpeg-turbo writes them (Pillow's and sharp's bytes), no longer as `image/jpeg` does: at the same quality, pixels a level or so apart, files of about the same size.
- `Flatten` of an image whose colours are not multiplied by alpha (a PNG, a WebP; most images), and JPEG output of an image with transparency (flattened over black), take the colours as libvips does, `(colour×alpha + background×(255−alpha)) / 255` with the fraction dropped, so that they are sharp's to the byte; they were a level apart on some pixels.
- A CMYK file (JPEG, TIFF) without a colour profile is converted to sRGB through libvips's CMYK profile, as libvips and sharp convert it, no longer with `color.CMYK`'s plain formula: printing inks come out as printed (full cyan is RGB 0,153,228, no longer 0,255,255). A `*image.CMYK` given to `FromImage` keeps `color.CMYK`'s values.

### Changed

- PNG at the default level, 7, is compressed with a port of libdeflate's lazy parsing (its level 7, to the byte), the same with every Go version, instead of `compress/flate`. Against Go 1.27's level 7, on 126 images: 1.8% smaller (geometric mean; from 28% smaller to 2.5% larger), in 19% more time end to end on one core. Paletted images (after colour reduction, or from a paletted source) gain most, as compress/flate's level 7 did poorly on their rows: 256-colour PNGs of photographs 2.4–3% smaller, of screenshots 4.6–8%, of smooth gradients 3–25% (one without dithering 2.3 times smaller). The same file whatever the number of threads and the memory limit. With several threads and a tight memory limit, PNG compression at levels 7 to 9 now waits for a block to be done where it failed for want of room for another. The memory the compressor takes is counted in the limit, and is sized for the image: a 150×103 image needs 0.8MB, a large one 2.3MB.
- `FastShrinkOnLoad` on a JPEG (and on a lossy WebP): the rest of the resize, which scales the Y, Cb and Cr planes, runs with AVX2 (NEON on arm64): the products of the 14-bit weights and the samples in pairs of 16-bit multiplications, eight destination samples at a time across and sixteen down, the same whole numbers, so the same bytes. A 6000×4000 JPEG fitted into 1536 is decoded and resized in 70ms instead of 106ms on one core (sharp: 77ms), and written as a WebP thumbnail in 140ms instead of 179ms (sharp, at its default effort, which is 4 to this module's 5: 149ms); a 4:4:4 one, to a JPEG, in 160ms instead of 212ms.
- GIF output and PNG colour reduction compare colours as libimagequant (pngquant's library, which sharp uses) does: in its gamma (each channel to the power 1.21) and with green weighted above red and blue (1, 0.5, 0.45), where all three counted alike; and the dithering passes on an error larger than 10 levels at three quarters, as libimagequant damps its large errors, so that they no longer add up over the pixels after them into specks of the wrong colour. On 12 photographs reduced to 256 colours as GIF, SSIMULACRA2 75.6 → 81.3 and butteraugli 1.66 → 1.07, files 2% smaller (sharp: 76.9 and 1.64); the 8-megapixel photograph as PNG of 256 colours 70.9 → 78.5, 4% smaller, and at a quarter of its size 82.3 against pngquant's 79.5 in an 11% smaller file; an animated GIF reduced to 128 colours 76.8 → 82.8. PSNR, which counts the channels alike, can fall by up to 1.5dB. 1–2% more time.
- JPEG files are written as libjpeg-turbo writes them, to the byte (sharp's files as they are, Pillow's without their JFIF segment): libjpeg's colour conversion, downsampling, accurate integer forward DCT, dummy blocks past the edges, optimized Huffman tables and markers, at the same speed. A resolution is written as JFIF 1.01, as libjpeg writes it.
- `FastShrinkOnLoad` applies to a lossy WebP without alpha too: reduced to a third or less, it is decoded at twice the size of the result, as libwebp scales while decoding, and the kernel does the rest (a 6000×4000 one to fit 400×400 in 0.39s instead of 0.74s, 48–51dB from the result without the option).
- `FastShrinkOnLoad` reduces the blocks with libjpeg's reduced inverse DCTs (`jidctred.c`) instead of averages worked out afresh, at the same speed: the planes of a 4:4:4 or grey JPEG are those of libjpeg's scaled decoding (Pillow's `draft`), and the result as close to a resize of the full image (46.7–50.5dB).
- JPEG files are decoded as libjpeg-turbo decodes them, and so Pillow and sharp, to the byte (its accurate integer inverse DCT, its fancy upsampling of subsampled chroma, its YCbCr to RGB tables), instead of as `image/jpeg` does: subsampled colour edges are smoother (a 4:2:2 photograph 0.36 dB closer to the original), and every operation that gives Pillow's or sharp's pixels does so on JPEG input too. Colour JPEG strips are RGBA rather than YCbCr, but where a JPEG goes straight into a JPEG or lossy WebP file.
- TIFF decoding: LZW 1.5 times faster (a 6000×4000 RGBA image in 0.45s instead of 0.68s; libtiff 0.31s), with the same bytes and errors as `x/image` on any input (fuzzed), and Deflate blocks read 64KB at a time (0.26s instead of 0.30s; libtiff 0.27s).
- `FitCover` puts a centred crop where sharp does: when the image is an odd number of pixels larger than the box, the odd pixel is cut on the left (top) rather than the right (bottom).
- JPEG encoding: the SIMD kernels write the quantized coefficients in zig-zag order themselves, and the mask of the nonzero ones is taken with AVX2: a 6000×4000 photograph at quality 85 in 87ms instead of 100ms on one core, with optimized tables 151ms instead of 168ms.
- JPEG encoding with AVX2: the forward DCT works in 16-bit lanes, two rows of samples to a register, as libjpeg-turbo's jfdctint-avx2 does (pairs of samples multiplied and added in one instruction), where it worked in 32-bit lanes with a slower multiply; the quantization divides as libjpeg-turbo's jquanti-avx2 divides, with two 16-bit high multiplies, which a test checks give the rounded quotient for every coefficient up to 32767 and every quantization value; the zig-zag order is made with byte shuffles of the rows in registers, where 64 coefficients were moved one at a time. The same bytes. A 6000×4000 RGB image in memory, quality 85: 70ms instead of 74ms on one core, 107ms instead of 112ms with optimized tables.
- Lossless WebP decoding: each pixel goes into the colour cache as it is decoded (a literal at once, a copy once copied), where the pixels since the last cache lookup were entered before each one, and a pixel taken from the cache is not entered again (it is already where it would go); a code of a single symbol (the alpha of an opaque image) is taken without a lookup, so that the next code does not wait for one; the inverse predictors 0 to 4 and 8 to 12 run four pixels at a time with AVX on amd64, after libwebp's SSE2 code (Select with a sum of absolute differences, the clamped one in 16-bit lanes), and the green is added back two pixels at a time. The same pixels. A 6000×4000 photograph: 0.31s instead of 0.42s on one core, where libwebp 1.6's `dwebp` with its SIMD takes 0.32s; a 2.5-megapixel one 24ms instead of 32ms (25ms).
- Progressive JPEG: the scan search codes every candidate scan of every block going from one coefficient that is not zero at the scan's bit plane to the next, as libjpeg-turbo's encoder does, instead of looking at each coefficient in turn; which ones those are is worked out without a branch (with AVX2 on amd64, 64 coefficients at once), as is the last nonzero coefficient of each block. The same bytes. A 6000×4000 RGB image in memory, quality 85: 0.75s instead of 1.85s on one core (mozjpeg's search through sharp: 1.17s).
- An image in memory (`FromImage`) is encoded without copying its rows: it is handed on in strips of 16 rows (where 16 rows take no more than 1MB), so that the bands of the JPEG and WebP encoders lie inside one strip and are passed on as views of it, where they were copied out of strips of 5 rows; whether an `*image.RGBA` or `*image.NRGBA` has transparency is checked eight pixels at a time, and the JPEG encoder asks only of NRGBA images, for which it matters. A 6000×4000 `*image.RGBA` written as JPEG (quality 85) in 74ms instead of 87ms on one core. The bytes are the same.
- Lossy WebP decoding: the coefficients are read by assembly on amd64 and arm64 (plain integer instructions, so also with Go 1.26 on arm64; the purego tag keeps the Go code), the range renormalized from tables after every bit instead of only when it falls below 127, which took an unpredictable branch, and the bits that end a block or skip a zero going straight to their code; near the end of the read window, where a block could need bytes not yet read, the Go code reads it as before, so errors come at the same rows. A block whose only coefficient is the DC (about half of them in a photograph) is transformed by adding the DC, with AVX2 or NEON, as libwebp does, where every block with a coefficient went through the whole inverse transform; the DC, vertical and horizontal predictions write whole rows at once. On amd64 the loop filter works as libwebp's SSE2 code does, in 8-bit lanes with saturating arithmetic, the three inner edges of a macroblock's luma in one call that keeps the columns (or rows) it has loaded for the next edge, where each edge was loaded, widened to 16 bits and transposed on its own: a third of the time. Every pixel is the same. A 6000×4000 photograph (quality 80) decodes on one core in 0.25s instead of 0.32s (`dwebp`, libwebp 1.6 with its SIMD: 0.256s), with the purego tag in 0.51s instead of 0.53s; shrunk on load to fit 400×400, 0.27s instead of 0.39s.
- `Modulate` keeps what it has turned each colour into, in a direct-mapped cache of 32K colours per thread (256KB, within the memory limit): a photograph has a few hundred thousand colours among its millions of pixels, and nine in ten are looked up rather than worked out again, the same pixels. The JPEG encoder takes opaque NRGBA strips (from `Modulate`, sharpening, CLAHE and other operations) as RGBA again, the same bytes, as re-cutting them into bands had lost that they are opaque. A 6000×4000 photograph with `Modulate(1.1, 1.3, 20)`, single-threaded: 0.41s instead of 0.71s.
- JPEG encoding: the Huffman codes and value bits of a block go into a 64-bit buffer that goes out eight bytes at a time (as libjpeg-turbo's put_buffer), for the standard and for optimized tables, the same bytes: a 6000×4000 photograph at quality 85 in 78ms instead of 85ms on one core, with optimized tables 118ms instead of 151ms (libjpeg-turbo through Pillow: 66ms and 167ms). Lossy WebP decoding reads its bits as libwebp's VP8GetBitAlt does (no renormalizing while the range stays large), 1% faster.
- Lossy WebP at effort 1 is no longer the same as effort 2: it picks the 16x16 and chroma predictors by the error of their prediction (and the cost of the mode) and codes only those, as libwebp's methods 0 to 2 pick them, and codes two rather than four 4x4 modes of each block. A 6000×4000 photograph in 1.52s instead of 2.12s on one core; on 14 images, files 2.5% larger at the same SSIMULACRA2 and 4.0% at the same butteraugli than effort 2.
- Lossy WebP from colours (PNG, resized or flattened images; not a JPEG's Y'CbCr): sharp YUV shares the columns of each step among the helpers and runs its upsampling and corrections with AVX2 or NEON, every sample libwebp's as before: a 6000×4000 photograph flattened onto white, at quality 75, in 4.7s instead of 5.2s on one core, 1.2s instead of 1.8s with 8 threads.
- Lossy WebP decoding: the inverse transform with AVX2 or NEON (the kernel the encoder reconstructs with), every byte the same: a 6000×4000 photograph decodes in 0.33s instead of 0.36s on one core.
- `Blur`, the unsharp mask, `Median(3)` and `Trim` with AVX2 or NEON, every byte the same: the box passes of the blur (Pillow's integer arithmetic) across a row four channels at a time and down the rows eight columns at a time, the 3×3 median 32 bytes at a time. Rotation by any angle reads the pixels it interpolates straight from the tile they are in. A 6000×4000 photograph, single-threaded: `Blur(2)` adds 0.14s instead of 0.48s (Pillow 0.38s), `Sharpen(SharpenOptions{Sigma: 2})` 0.22s instead of 0.57s (0.48s), `Trim(10)` 0.09s instead of 0.91s, `RotateBy(30)` 0.51s instead of 1.13s (with the encoding of its larger output).
- The default resize (`x/image`'s arithmetic, in float64) with AVX2 or NEON: the sums down and across take the four channels of a pixel at once, each product rounded and then added (never fused) in the order of the Go code, and RGBA rows are turned into float64 the same way; every sum is the same to the bit, so the pixels are too. A 6000×4000 JPEG fitted into 1536×1536 and written as a JPEG in 0.17s instead of 0.25s on one core; 1080p enlarged to 4K in 0.09s instead of 0.12s.
- Lossy WebP encoding: the 4x4 forward transform (after libwebp's FTransform_SSE2), the inverse transform, the quantization and the squared error of the mode search, and the histogram of coefficients of the analysis, run with AVX2 or NEON; the trellis keeps the two nodes of each coefficient in registers with the path to each as a bit mask, and counts the bits of the block as it goes; the bits of a block are summed without branches. Every number is that of the Go code, so the files are the same: a 6000×4000 photograph at quality 80 in 4.4s instead of 6.5s on one core at the default effort, 2.6s instead of 4.0s at effort 1.
- `Resize` with the default method keeps 16-bit samples: a 16-bit PNG or TIFF is scaled to 16-bit strips (`*image.RGBA64`, `*image.Gray16`), the values `x/image`'s `Scale` gives such a destination, and written as 16 bits; it was cut to 8 bits.
- PNG encoding: the five filters of each row (and the sums by which libpng's heuristic chooses one) are worked out with AVX2 or NEON, the same row and choice as before: a 6000×4000 photograph to PNG (level 7) in 1.43s instead of 1.73s on one core.
- NEON on arm64 (with Go 1.27 or later; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): every AVX2 kernel (JPEG decoding and encoding, lossy WebP decoding, the search of loop filter levels, `MethodLibvips`' reduce) has a NEON version, every byte that of the Go code.
- Lossy WebP at effort 3 weighs the 4x4 modes without the trellis and keeps it for the mode chosen (libwebp's method 5): 15% faster than effort 4, which it was the same as, for files about 1% larger at the same SSIMULACRA2 and butteraugli (14 images).
- Lossy WebP encoding with AVX2 (amd64; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): the search of loop filter levels filters each macroblock with the decoder's AVX2 loop filter and takes the SSIM sums of its windows with AVX2, every number that of the Go code, so the files are the same: a 6000×4000 photograph at quality 80 in 6.5s instead of 8.3s on one core.
- Resizing with AVX2 (amd64; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): `MethodLibvips`'s reduce down and across, and libjpeg's 4×4 inverse DCT of a JPEG shrunk on load, run as assembly, every byte that of the Go code (and so sharp's); NRGBA strips of an image without transparency are encoded as JPEG through the same conversion as RGBA. A 6000×4000 JPEG fitted into 1536×1536 (Lanczos 3, `FastShrinkOnLoad`) and written as a JPEG: 109ms instead of 196ms on one core (sharp: 92ms).
- JPEG encoding with AVX2 (amd64; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): the conversion of RGB to YCbCr, the downsampling of the chroma, the forward DCT and the quantization run as assembly, every byte that of the Go code; and the first pass of optimized Huffman tables stages the symbols (a byte each) instead of a scan coded with the standard tables, which the second pass had to decode. A 6000×4000 photograph at quality 85: 100ms instead of 213ms on one core (libjpeg-turbo through Pillow: 66ms); with optimized tables 168ms instead of 388ms (167ms).
- JPEG decoding with AVX2 (amd64; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): the inverse DCT and the conversion of colour, with the upsampling of the chroma, run as assembly after libjpeg-turbo's SIMD code, every byte that of the Go code: a 6000×4000 photograph (quality 90) decodes on one core in 99ms (4:2:0), 109ms (4:2:2) and 126ms (4:4:4) instead of 208, 226 and 250ms, as fast as libjpeg-turbo with its SIMD (through Pillow: 101, 110 and 121ms).
- JPEG decoding is 45% faster, with the same pixels: the entropy-coded data is read 64 bits at a time and decoded through 11-bit tables that give most coefficients' values together with their codes, coefficients are dequantized as they are decoded, and the inverse DCT skips rows of zeros (a 6000×4000 baseline photograph in 98ms instead of 188ms on one core). With `FastShrinkOnLoad` the image is decoded straight to 1/2, 1/4 or 1/8 of its size from the coefficients, as libjpeg does, instead of being decoded in full and averaged, and the rest of the resize scales its Y, Cb and Cr planes as they are, in fixed point, instead of converting to RGBA: shrinking that photograph to fit 400×400 takes 82ms instead of 231ms (libvips' `vipsthumbnail`: 97ms), and to fit 1536×1536 157ms instead of 320ms (148ms; with 2 threads 108ms against its 172ms).

Shrunk by 8 with `FastShrinkOnLoad`, where each block becomes the mean of its pixels, only the scans of DC coefficients are decoded: that photograph shrunk to fit 400×400 in 50ms instead of 745ms (libvips' `vipsthumbnail`: 228ms). An end-of-band run that a corrupt scan leaves unfinished now ends with the scan, as in libjpeg; the standard library carries it into the next scan.
- JPEG encoding is more than twice as fast, writing the same bytes as before (those of the standard library's `jpeg.Encode`): the entropy-coded data is gathered 64 bits at a time and written 32 at a time, a block's bits kept in registers; only the coefficients that are not zero are visited, found four at a time; quantization has no branch; the colour conversion of RGBA and NRGBA pixels is done inline; and the chroma of a YCbCr image subsampled 4:2:0 (as decoded JPEGs mostly are) is taken as it is instead of being spread over every pixel and averaged back (4:4:4: averaged straight from its planes). A 6000×4000 JPEG re-encoded on one core: 0.23s instead of 0.54s (libvips: 0.12s; with 2 threads 0.13s against its 0.13s, with 4 threads 0.10s).
- PNG images and Deflate-compressed TIFF images are decompressed about twice as fast (`internal/inflate`, written with libdeflate's method: a 64-bit bit buffer refilled without a branch, tables that give a code's extra bits with it, the next code looked up before the refill, matches copied eight bytes at a time), accepting and rejecting the same data as before and reading as much of the file. A 6000×4000 RGB PNG is decoded in 0.25s instead of 0.37s on one core (libvips: 0.25s); converted to JPEG, 0.39s instead of 0.51s (0.25s).

A 6000×4000 photograph: 7.5s instead of 10.1s on one core, 1.55s instead of 1.79s with 8 threads.
- Lossy WebP decoding with AVX2 (amd64; the `purego` build tag leaves it out): the loop filter, and the upsampling and conversion of colour, run as assembly after libwebp's SSE2 code, every byte that of the Go code: a 6000×4000 photograph decodes on one core in 0.36s instead of 0.57s, faster than libwebp 1.6 with its SIMD (0.50s through Pillow).
- Lossy WebP decoding is 15–18% faster, with the same pixels: the bits are read as libwebp's VP8GetBit reads them, and a block's coefficients with the reader held in local variables (a 6000×4000 image at quality 95: 0.78s instead of 0.95s; dwebp 0.48s).
- Lossy WebP decoding shares its work when there are cores for it (`Threads` of 4 or more): the loop filter of each row of macroblocks runs while the next row is decoded, as libwebp's threaded decoder does, and the conversion to RGB is shared out; the pixels are the same. A 6000×4000 photograph shrunk to fit 1536×1536 in 0.49s instead of 0.57s with 4 threads.
- Lossless WebP decoding (and the compressed alpha of lossy WebP) takes 25–65% less time, with the same pixels and the same failures as `golang.org/x/image/webp`: the Huffman codes are decoded through tables, as libwebp decodes them (a code that is not complete keeps the tree), 64 bits of input at a time, and the predictor transform works on a run of pixels four channels at a time (a 1600×1097 photograph in 31ms instead of 42ms on one core, a 1001×687 one in 15ms instead of 22ms; libwebp's `dwebp`: 30ms and 13ms, 25ms and 11ms with its SIMD).
- A YCbCr or NYCbCrA image with chroma subsampled vertically and its top edge above 0 at an odd row no longer makes JPEG encoding (or anything that re-cuts it into bands) fail with an internal error.
- From level 7, PNG output tries no filter against the filters the rows choose, every 32KB of rows, and keeps the smaller: screenshots, icons and drawings 10–19% smaller; photographs unchanged.
- PNG levels 8 and 9 search for the smallest file: rows are filtered as oxipng's Brute strategy filters them (every filter tried), and the data is compressed with a Go port of libdeflate's near-optimal parsing instead of `compress/flate`, its blocks split by cost and its symbols priced at their entropy, as zopfli does. On 17 screenshots, photographs and game artwork, 12.2% (8) and 13.5% (9) smaller than level 7, against 12.8% for oxipng `-o max` (in a sixth of its time) and 13.8% for ECT `-9`; the same file with every Go version. They take about 4MB (8) and 6.5MB (9) more memory per thread.

A still image passes as before. So does `MaxFileSize` with `Animated()` on an animation, which was ignored.

- Lossy WebP: segment-based quantization, following libwebp's spatial noise shaping.
- Lossy WebP: `WebPOptions.Effort` applies to lossy output too (it was for lossless only). On 14 images: 6.6% / 9.5% smaller than libwebp's default and 1.3% / 2.2% smaller than its best settings (`-m 6 -af -sharp_yuv`), at equal SSIMULACRA2 / butteraugli. Encoding takes 2 to 2.6 times as long; `Effort: 1` keeps the earlier speed and is still 6.1% / 4.8% smaller than libwebp's default.
- Lossy WebP with transparency: the alpha plane is coded by the lossless encoder at its default effort, through the better of no filter and the horizontal one (all four filters from effort 7), with the optimal parse for planes of up to 4 megapixels (all from effort 7), and the colours of transparent areas are made cheap, as libwebp does: a photograph in a soft round window 50% smaller (146KB to 73KB at quality 75; libwebp 88KB), alpha chunks up to 6 times smaller (Tux's 2% smaller than libwebp's). Up to 8MB more memory while the plane is coded; under a low memory limit the alpha chunk (not the pixels) can differ, as lossless files can.
- Lossy WebP is decoded to the pixels libwebp, and so browsers, show, to the byte: chroma interpolated between samples and libwebp's integer conversion (before, each chroma sample covered its 2×2 block and the colours differed by about half a level on average, up to 20–40 at colour edges). Decoded lossy WebP is now RGB; `Metadata()` still reports it as `ycbcr`.
- Lossless WebP chooses its LZ77 matches by the cheapest path through the pixels (the idea of libwebp’s TraceBackwards), searches further for repeats in screenshots, and tries several block sizes and numbers of Huffman groups: 5.5% smaller over 17 images at the default effort (screenshots 6–9%), in 3.8 times the time (42s against 11s on one core; libwebp's default takes 24s); 5.3% smaller in total than libwebp’s slowest setting (5.7% at effort 9, in 106s against its 308s). At every pixel the longest match at the nearest distances above and to the left is offered besides the hash chain's (a gradient mask 16% smaller), and prefix codes are evened out for run-length coding of their lengths when that pays, as libwebp does. Images of up to 4 megapixels also race a variant with libwebp's cross-color transform (icons and interface pictures with colour gradients 2–9% smaller), their split into Huffman groups is also tried without the colour cache (an icon 4% smaller), and their variants without the predictor also try matches only at distance 1 and a row up, as libwebp's "RLE" references (icons of few colours 3–4% smaller). A 6000×4000 photograph from a JPEG, whose noise leaves the parse little to find, comes out the same size, in 5.1s instead of 4.1s and at most 25.6MB instead of 22.2MB.
- Lossless WebP selects each band's predictors using the cost model as it stands at the start of that band, so that they can be selected in parallel; output files differ slightly.
- The command-line code has moved from `cmd/goimgtools` to package `cli`; `cmd/goimgtools` builds the same program.

### Fixed

- Lossy WebP at effort 5 and above (the default): the frame's loop filter level was written as that of the first segment, which the measuring often sets to 0 (the segment of the most textured macroblocks); decoders (libwebp, libvpx, this module's) then filter nothing in the whole frame, whatever the other segments' levels. It is now the highest of the segments' levels, which are written as they are. Smooth photographs at low sizes lost their loop filter: on one such photograph and 19 edited versions of it at quality 75, SSIMULACRA2 69.3 → 73.6 and butteraugli 1.28 → 1.19, the files the same size.
- `Extend` and `FitContain` of an image with transparency kept as NRGBA (a PNG with alpha) went through premultiplied 8-bit RGBA, which moved the colours of translucent pixels (by up to 255/alpha levels: 4 at alpha 55); they keep them now.
- `FastShrinkOnLoad` on a JPEG whose components are sampled in ways the decoder does not reduce itself (Go's test files 121121, 211211, 221122, 222112) failed with "shrink: unsupported pixel type"; the decoded image is averaged again.

- A compressed TIFF of a large image was one strip, which libvips (and so sharp) refuses once its compressed data exceeds 50MB: compressed images of more than 1MB of pixel data are now written in strips of about 1MB.
- On processors that fuse a multiplication and an addition into one instruction (ARM64, such as Apple silicon and AWS Graviton, POWER, s390x, RISC-V, LoongArch), some results could differ from those on x86 by a level or a pixel: `Linear`, the blend modes, `Convolve` with fractional weights, the palettes of GIF and few-colour PNG, the Mitchell kernel, watermarks placed by ratio, the size of `Rotate`, `Blur`'s boxes, text outlines, and choices made by the lossless WebP and jpegli encoders. Every product is now rounded before it is added, as on x86, and CI checks that the compiler fuses none of them on those processors.
- A panic in an operation could leave the chain's input open, retaining memory budget and temporary files.
- `Frames().LoopCount` of a GIF was its repeat count, one less than the number of times it plays, which is what the field documents.

Initial public release: reading and writing PNG, JPEG, WebP, GIF (including animation), BMP and TIFF a few rows at a time within a memory limit; resizing (six kernels, five fit modes), cropping, rotation by multiples of 90°, flipping, flattening, compositing and watermarks, text; JPEG with optimized coding tables and progressive encoding, PNG colour reduction, `MaxFileSize`; batch processing; extension interfaces (`Pipe`, `Pixels`, `Apply`, `FromImage`, `FromSource`, `RegisterFormat`, `Stream`, `ToImage`, package `pipeline`); the `info`, `check`, `compare` and `formats` commands, and package `inspect`; input and output pixel limits, cancellation, and panics returned as errors.
